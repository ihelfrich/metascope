What's actually hiding in a photo

Every photo your phone takes is wrapped in a layer of invisible data called metadata. You never see it, but anyone you send the file to can. Here is what it routinely contains.

  • Your exact location. Most phones stamp the GPS coordinates of where a photo was taken, accurate to a few meters. A photo of your kitchen carries your home address. A photo of your kid in the yard carries their school's address if you took one there earlier and the camera cached it.
  • Your device. Make, model, and often a unique serial number that ties every photo you have ever posted back to the same camera.
  • The exact moment. Date and time down to the second, sometimes with the timezone, which quietly reveals where in the world you were.
  • The editing trail. What software touched the file, and sometimes a history of edits.
  • A hidden thumbnail. This is the one that surprises people. Cameras embed a small preview image inside the file. On some phones and editors, cropping the visible photo does not replace that embedded preview, so the thumbnail can still show the original, uncropped picture. People have leaked exactly the thing they thought they cropped out.

Why this tool is safe to use

MetaScope reads every byte in your browser. No file, no thumbnail, no coordinate is ever sent anywhere. To prove the point: it refuses to load a map of your location automatically, because fetching a map tile would hand your coordinates to a map server, which is the exact leak this tool exists to stop. It only opens a map if you click and confirm.

Scrubbing

The scrub re-encodes each image from its raw pixels, which discards every metadata block, including the hidden thumbnail. The picture looks the same; the baggage is gone. For PDFs it clears the document properties (author, title, producer, creation tool).
